# Directing with a generator
### Version for HKU — MA in AI Filmmaking and Media
*Xinrui Shao · case study · 2026*

---

## 1. Context

I directed *Demon*, a ~20-minute narrative short made with a text-to-video model: cold realism,
hand-held, no score, no subtitles. The storyboard runs to 18 scenes and 210 shots, parsed from my own
script and shot table.

I did not come to this as a technologist. I came to it as a director who wanted a specific film —
a world cold enough that a single wrong detail would break it — and found that the model could not
be trusted to remember what the world was.

## 2. The real problem was continuity, not capability

A generator can make almost any single shot look plausible. That is not where the film was lost.
In a film, everything is an obligation to something earlier: a door that must stay the same door, a
chain on a wrist that must not become handcuffs, a face that must survive a change of clothes, a wet
floor that must not read as blood. The model holds none of this between shots.

So the problem was not "how do I prompt better". It was: **how do I hold a world in place across
210 shots when the tool that makes the shots has no memory at all.** That is a production problem,
and production problems have production answers.

## 3. How I divided authorship with the machine

The rule I set on day one: **I write the rules, I judge the result, the model executes.**

- The model renders a shot from a prompt and reference plates.
- I decide what the shot must contain, approve the prompt, generate the clip in my own tool, watch it,
  and call it usable or not.
- The crew — seven agent skills I built — does the paperwork: expand continuity, check the prompt
  against past failures, keep the asset document, count the rework.

Two points are never automated: **approve the prompt** and **judge the clip**. I also refused to let
an agent drive the render queue, even though I could have: the round-trip cost more than it saved, and
it put latency between my eyes and the next prompt. Automating the wrong thing is a creative decision,
not a technical one.

## 4. What I did as a director

**Assets before shots.** Faces first. Each character's identity prompt pins the face in detail —
bone structure, brow, eyes, nose, mouth, chin, skin, hair, body, hands, and the marks that make it
unmistakable (a mole under the left eye, a broken scar in one eyebrow, missing teeth). Every later
costume change is a *variant* prompt that describes only what changed and says: copy the identity
plate, same person. Then locations: four directional views per space, written against a single layout
note, so a room cannot change shape when the camera turns around. That is the AI equivalent of a
floor plan, a lighting plot and a continuity still.

**Reverse locks as aesthetic discipline.** Every prompt names what must *not* happen: no second light
source, no duplicated furniture, no extra hand, no auto push-in, this is not handcuffs, this is water
and not blood. Roughly half of my rework traced back to a missing lock. The negative space of a shot
is part of directing it.

**A failure catalogue as a director's notebook.** Fifteen entries, each one paid for by a regenerated
take, each carrying the wording that fixed it. When the model improves, I will re-earn them; until
then they are the difference between taste and shots that survive.

**The rule that saved the film's grammar:** no cross-shot references, ever. "Same as before" is not an
instruction a generator can obey. Every prompt re-describes the world in full — which is also, as it
turns out, how you discover what your world actually is.

## 6. One decision I would defend in a viva

One scene in the script would have required depicting the sexual abuse of a 12-year-old. I ruled it
out of production before shooting started: no prompt, no storyboard, no reference image, no
compromise. What the film does instead is refuse to show it — the cut passes over the night, the audio
carries the aftermath, the next scene plays on the child's behaviour — and the film's most important
beat (the perpetrator stopping) is carried by a reaction, not by an act. The boundary was written into
the project's content policy and never renegotiated shot by shot.

I include this in a portfolio piece deliberately. A director who cannot say where the film must not go
does not have a method; they have a habit.

## 7. Reflection

- **Assets first, always.** Early shots that improvised a look had to be re-shot once the identity
  plate existed.
- **Write the failure down the first time you see it.** A tendency noticed on take 1 and written into
  the catalogue saves take 3 of every later shot that shares it.
- **Count from day one.** Attribution — prompt defect, model limitation, asset problem — is the only
  thing that tells you whether to rewrite, redesign or remake.
- **Keep the human where judgement lives, and nowhere else.** The two gates are not a limitation of
  the workflow; they are the workflow.

Studying film production taught me that the director's job is specification and judgement. Making a
film with a generator did not change that — it made it visible, because there is no crew to absorb the
ambiguity. What I built is the crew: not to make the film instead of me, but to make my own standards
survive contact with a tool that does not share them.
